# Human Cognition as a Strategic Asset in the Emerging AI–Security–Industrial Economy
## Why cognitive capability may become more important precisely as AI becomes more capable

**I think human cognition could become a central determinant of strategic success in the emerging economy—but its economic role is changing.**

The key distinction is between **using intelligence to perform tasks within an established system** and **using intelligence to determine which systems, objectives and opportunities are worth committing to**.

As AI increasingly handles analysis, prediction, content production and operational optimisation, the second function may become disproportionately important.

This connects directly with the MRQ–Trident-G framework we've been developing.

> **In a strategically integrating economy, the crucial human cognitive capability may be the ability to recognise emerging structures of opportunity, construct sufficiently accurate models of them, make appropriate commitments, and revise those models when reality changes.**

This is not simply a matter of increasing IQ, improving productivity or becoming more adaptable in general.

It concerns **strategic cognitive agency**: the capacity to understand changing constraints, identify viable possibilities and direct resources towards chosen outcomes.

I would identify three reasons cognition becomes especially important.

First, the five strategic sectors—AI, energy, defence, advanced manufacturing and finance—are increasingly interconnected. Executives need to understand dependencies and second-order consequences, not merely their own industries.

Second, AI can make established operating models more efficient, but greater efficiency does not establish that the models themselves remain appropriate. Human judgement remains important for identifying omitted variables, inappropriate objectives and structural changes.

Third, a system increasingly mediated by algorithms can shape the assumptions of the people participating in it. Human cognitive capability therefore matters not only for competing successfully but for retaining the ability to question the criteria by which success is defined.

The implication is that **human cognition is both a productive resource within the strategic economy and a potential safeguard against its maladaptive lock-in**.

## 1. The strategic economy creates five major cognitive demands

I would distinguish five cognitive functions that become particularly important when organisations must make substantial commitments within changing economic structures.

| Cognitive capability | Strategic function | Example in the emerging economy |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Situational intelligence** | Recognise which environmental changes matter | Identify how energy constraints, AI investment and defence procurement interact |
| **2. Causal reasoning** | Understand what produces outcomes and distinguish symptoms from underlying mechanisms | Determine whether falling profits reflect pricing, technology, supply chains or structural changes in demand |
| **3. Strategic possibility generation** | Identify feasible opportunities that existing models overlook | Recognise an opportunity to supply grid infrastructure rather than compete directly in AI |
| **4. Adaptive judgement and commitment** | Choose when to investigate, invest, persist, renegotiate or withdraw | Commit capital to an emerging strategic market while limiting unacceptable dependencies |
| **5. Cognitive resilience and model reconstruction** | Remain effective under disruption and revise assumptions when circumstances change | Reconstruct a strategy following sanctions, technological displacement or the failure of a major supplier |

These are not entirely independent capacities. Their interaction is particularly important.

For example, an executive may possess excellent analytical abilities but generate a very narrow set of strategic alternatives. Another may be creative but fail to recognise which opportunities are economically feasible.

**The strategic advantage comes from coordinating cognitive capabilities appropriately for the situation.**

This is closely related to your Trident-G distinction between underlying capacity and intelligent deployment of that capacity.

## 2. The pivotal shift: from cognitive production to cognitive governance

I think AI is creating a useful distinction between two broad economic functions.

### Cognitive production

This involves generating analyses, forecasts, reports, designs, software, recommendations and operational decisions.

AI can increasingly perform or assist with many of these activities.

The resulting commercial advantage may be substantial, particularly when organisations combine AI with appropriate expertise and workflows.

For example, a preregistered field experiment involving **791 professionals at Procter & Gamble** found that generative AI improved performance on product-innovation tasks and helped integrate technical and commercial perspectives. Individuals working with AI achieved performance comparable to teams working without it, for the tasks studied. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.20702?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### Cognitive governance

This concerns a different set of questions.

**Which problem should we solve?**

**What would count as a successful outcome?**

**Which assumptions are appropriate?**

**What should we believe when our models disagree?**

**Which opportunities deserve irreversible commitments?**

**When should we fundamentally reconstruct the model?**

AI can contribute to these functions too. There is no reason to assume that advanced AI will remain incapable of strategic reasoning.

But organisational authority, accountability, legitimacy and the consequences of decisions remain important reasons to retain meaningful human governance.

The emerging challenge is not to reserve every difficult cognitive task for a human.

It is to design systems in which human and artificial intelligence produce better judgements together.

That complementarity is not automatic. A 2025 review of 52 empirical studies of human–AI teaming in clinical settings found that AI frequently improved clinician performance, but combined performance did not consistently exceed the better-performing partner. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44387-025-00052-4?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**The important strategic capability is therefore not simply human intelligence or AI intelligence, but effective human–AI cognitive coordination.**

# 3. The distinction between execution intelligence and strategic intelligence

This connects directly with our Attractor C discussion.

Imagine an executive team that has exceptional access to AI models, financial dashboards, market forecasts and automated operating systems.

It becomes increasingly effective at improving performance within its established business model.

But suppose the organisation's actual market position is becoming structurally vulnerable.

A competitor has secured essential infrastructure.

New government procurement requirements exclude the business from an emerging market.

A key technology provider is increasing switching costs.

An alternative value chain is becoming substantially more attractive.

The organisation may continue improving its existing operations while failing to recognise that the strategic problem has changed.

We therefore have two separate cognitive demands.

| | Execution intelligence | Strategic intelligence |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary task** | Perform effectively within an established operating model | Determine whether that operating model remains appropriate |
| **Cognitive emphasis** | Attention, working memory, efficiency and procedural knowledge | Causal reasoning, mental models, foresight and option generation |
| **Decision emphasis** | Optimisation | Selection and commitment |
| **Learning emphasis** | Improve established procedures | Identify when procedures, objectives or assumptions must change |
| **Typical failure** | Inefficiency, errors and poor execution | Committing resources to an increasingly inappropriate strategic position |
| **Role of AI** | Automation, prediction and operational support | Scenario generation, model criticism, evidence synthesis and decision support |

Neither is dispensable.

In the emerging strategic economy, **organisations need both**, but the value of strategic intelligence may increase when AI makes routine cognitive production cheaper.

This is consistent with the OECD's June 2026 assessment, which identifies critical thinking, creativity, problem-solving and managerial capabilities as important complements to AI adoption. It also notes that only a small minority of workers need advanced AI-development skills. [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/ai-and-skills_f843b352-en/full-report.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

# 4. A particularly important role: deciding what to commit to

Let's consider an example from the strategic economy.

Suppose a specialist engineering business has £5 million available for expansion.

It identifies three opportunities:

**A.** Expand its established consumer-industrial product line.

**B.** Develop specialised manufacturing capabilities for electricity-grid infrastructure.

**C.** Enter a defence-related supply chain through a partnership requiring significant certification and infrastructure investment.

All three could have attractive projected returns.

AI can assist with market analysis, cost estimates, forecasting and scenario modelling.

But the organisation must still evaluate several fundamentally different kinds of uncertainty.

| Cognitive challenge | Required strategic judgement |
|---|---|
| **Understanding structural change** | Is growing demand sufficiently durable to justify specialised capacity? |
| **Causal reasoning** | Which policy, technological or commercial developments will actually generate customer demand? |
| **Relational integration** | How do financing, procurement, energy costs, qualifications and market access interact? |
| **Opportunity evaluation** | Which investment creates the strongest combination of profitability and strategic positioning? |
| **Commitment calibration** | Should the business invest fully, stage the investment, establish a partnership or wait? |
| **Model criticism** | What evidence would show that the chosen opportunity was based on incorrect assumptions? |
| **Adaptive recovery** | How could the organisation respond if its chosen market develops differently from expectations? |

The critical outcome is not the quality of the analysis alone.

It is whether the organisation makes an appropriate commitment.

**This is where cognition becomes an economic asset: by influencing the selection, timing and governance of consequential capital allocations.**

A relatively small improvement in strategic judgement could have substantial financial consequences when applied to multimillion-pound decisions.

Conversely, a cognitively sophisticated organisation can still make poor decisions if management incentives, authority structures or external constraints prevent it from acting on good evidence.

That distinction is essential: cognitive capability has economic value only when people have sufficient opportunities and decision rights to deploy it.

# 5. Trident-G: why the relationship between capacity, strategy and reality matters

Your Trident-G architecture provides a useful interpretation of this problem.

Recall the three developmental levels:

### CAPACITY → STRATEGY → REALITY

**Capacity:** Can the person perform the relevant cognitive operations?

**Strategy:** Can they select and deploy those operations appropriately?

**Reality:** Does the resulting behaviour remain effective under actual environmental constraints?

I would extend this to executive decision-making.

| Trident-G level | Executive application | Potential outcome measure |
|---|---|---|
| **Capacity** | Reasoning, attentional control, relational integration and complex working memory | Performance on relevant cognitive tasks |
| **Adaptive strategy** | Recognising when to investigate, verify, commit or change direction | Decision calibration, appropriate strategy switching and error detection |
| **Reality coupling** | Applying strategies to consequential organisational decisions | Forecast accuracy, decision quality, profitability, resilience and successful strategic commitments |
| **Developmental learning** | Retaining what works while rebuilding models when necessary | Improvement across changing strategic contexts and delayed follow-up |

The fourth row introduces your longer-term **Gf → Gc → Gf** developmental cycle.

An organisation acquires experience, consolidates useful knowledge and then uses that knowledge as a foundation for further exploration.

The potential failure mode is becoming highly effective at deploying consolidated knowledge while progressively losing the capacity to generate alternatives.

In Trident-G terms, the problem is excessive exploitation relative to the demands of the changing environment.

**The goal is not continuous exploration. It is properly calibrated commitment, with the ability to reopen the model when evidence requires it.**

Your near-criticality framework provides a hypothesis about the regulation underlying such behaviour, but it does not yet establish that executive performance corresponds to a particular measurable neural criticality regime.

# 6. Cognitive resilience becomes a strategic requirement

I think cognitive resilience deserves particular emphasis.

In an economy with increasing technological interdependence and geopolitical uncertainty, decision-makers may encounter more situations involving:

- High information volume and competing priorities.
- Uncertain forecasts and contradictory evidence.
- Time pressure and costly decisions.
- Interruptions to established workflows.
- Abrupt changes in market and operating conditions.

Here we need to distinguish **cognitive capacity** from **cognitive resilience**.

Capacity concerns what someone can accomplish under particular conditions.

Resilience concerns whether they can maintain, recover or appropriately reorganise their cognitive functioning when those conditions change.

For strategic decision-making, that could involve maintaining situational awareness during disruption, recognising errors without becoming overwhelmed, restoring effective control following setbacks and switching strategies when new evidence arrives.

This provides a rationale for training under changing conditions rather than exclusively under familiar, predictable task requirements.

There is also an important organisational implication.

A company cannot compensate indefinitely for excessive workloads, poor information architecture or inadequate decision authority by training employees to tolerate more cognitive pressure.

**Cognitive resilience must be supported by appropriate organisational conditions.**

The August 2026 ILO report on changing skills in the AI era identifies cognitive, socioemotional and digital skills alongside adaptability, resilience and human agency as important dimensions of evolving work. [International Labour Organization](https://www.ilo.org/publications/changing-landscape-skills-age-ai?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The implication is that cognitive development and organisational design should reinforce one another.

# 7. The Attractor C paradox: the economy needs cognition while potentially narrowing it

This may be the most interesting connection to our earlier discussion.

The emerging economic architecture could produce two opposing developments.

### The demand for higher-order cognitive capability increases

Some organisations will require people capable of integrating information across disciplines, recognising emerging opportunities, questioning assumptions and making strategic commitments.

These capabilities are particularly important when AI-generated information becomes abundant.

### The environment may simultaneously reinforce narrower cognitive habits

AI systems and integrated platforms can make established workflows easier to follow.

Workers may increasingly respond to standardised objectives and recommendations.

Executives may receive strategic analyses produced through similar computational models.

Consumers may encounter opportunities filtered through a restricted set of platforms.

In our Attractor C hypothesis, repeated participation could encourage the internalisation of common decision rules and assumptions.

This creates the possibility that:

> **The economy increasingly rewards sophisticated strategic intelligence at the same time that many of its operating systems encourage reliance on externally supplied models, objectives and decision procedures.**

That is a hypothesis, not an established account of how all AI adoption affects cognition.

But there is relevant evidence.

A 2025 Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon survey of 319 knowledge workers found that greater confidence in generative AI was associated with less self-reported critical-thinking activity. The authors also found that AI shifted cognitive work towards verification, integrating outputs and overseeing tasks. This was correlational evidence, not proof of cognitive decline. [Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_x-social-details_comments-action_comment-text\&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The strategic challenge is therefore to **retain and develop human judgement while benefiting from cognitive automation**.

For an enterprise, this could become a genuine competitive issue.

An organisation that automates analysis without retaining the ability to question it may become operationally efficient but strategically dependent.

# 8. Human cognition as a form of strategic infrastructure

I would introduce a distinction between three kinds of infrastructure.

| Infrastructure | Principal function | Strategic importance |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical infrastructure** | Energy, manufacturing, transport and materials | Determines what can physically be produced and delivered |
| **Digital infrastructure** | Compute, AI, data, models and operational platforms | Determines how information is processed and activities coordinated |
| **Human cognitive infrastructure** | Distributed expertise, understanding, judgement, creativity, learning and decision authority | Determines how effectively objectives are selected, evidence evaluated and organisational capabilities redirected |

The third category is not simply an individual employee's intelligence.

It includes **the organisation's distributed cognitive capabilities**.

A capable engineering team, for example, may possess deep practical knowledge that is poorly captured in formal databases.

An experienced procurement specialist may recognise signs of supplier failure before they appear in conventional indicators.

A multidisciplinary executive team may recognise strategic relationships that are absent from its existing ontology.

These capabilities become economically important when the organisation can integrate them effectively.

That requires appropriate communication, decision rights, trust and opportunities for independent judgement.

It also requires capturing useful experience as organisational knowledge without eliminating the capacity to question that knowledge later.

### Collective cognition matters

The strategic resource is therefore not simply a collection of high-performing individuals.

It is an organisational capability to:

**Observe → Understand → Generate alternatives → Evaluate → Commit → Learn → Reconstruct.**

An enterprise with individually capable employees but poor coordination may still make weak decisions.

Conversely, an organisation with effective collective decision procedures may outperform another organisation with stronger individuals but fragmented knowledge.

This is one reason human cognition should be treated as part of enterprise architecture, rather than solely as a training or HR issue.

# 9. What should executives actually commit to developing?

Under our assumed strategic-economic trajectory, I would propose a **Human Cognitive Capability Strategy** alongside the organisation's AI strategy.

It should identify which cognitive capabilities are important to the chosen business position, how those capabilities interact with AI, and how their practical value will be measured.

I would organise this around four priorities.

| Strategic commitment | Practical implementation | Intended outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Develop strategically relevant cognitive capabilities** | Targeted development of reasoning, attention, adaptive control and relevant domain expertise | Better cognitive performance in important work activities |
| **Improve human–AI judgement** | Verification methods, confidence calibration, independent evaluation and clear decision rights | More accurate and accountable decisions |
| **Protect cognitive resilience** | Workload design, recovery, interruption management and appropriate cognitive training | Better functioning under demanding or changing conditions |
| **Institutionalise model reconstruction** | Competing hypotheses, causal review, scenario analysis and explicit strategic reopening criteria | Reduced risk of prolonged commitment to an inadequate model |

The important distinction is that these are **investments in organisational capability**, not simply purchases of cognitive-training software.

For example, a programme intended to improve executive reasoning should demonstrate benefits in relevant decision contexts.

Improvements on an isolated reasoning exercise would be useful evidence of task learning, but not sufficient evidence of better capital allocation.

A robust programme would compare organisational decisions and outcomes before and after an intervention, ideally against an appropriate control or alternative intervention.

# 10. The commercial implications for IQ Mindware and SWI

This analysis also helps clarify the value propositions of your two cognitive businesses.

They address different levels of the same economic problem.

### IQ Mindware / Synergy IQ: developing individual cognitive resources

The consumer proposition becomes particularly relevant to an AI-mediated economy in which people may need to maintain their capabilities while delegating more cognitive work.

Its proposed developmental architecture remains:

**Capacity training → Strategy calibration → Transfer-oriented practice → Real-world application.**

The strategically relevant benefits to investigate are cognitive performance, capacity, resilience, adaptive control and independent judgement.

Synergy IQ can support practice of component cognitive operations. Whether this produces improved executive decisions, workplace resilience or meaningful far transfer remains to be demonstrated.

### SWI: making human cognition operationally effective

SWI addresses a different question.

An organisation may contain highly capable people whose performance is constrained by poor workflows, misaligned AI systems, inadequate decision rights or ineffective organisational coordination.

Its proposed value is to identify those constraints and support evidence-qualified changes to people, technology and work systems.

That gives SWI a natural role in the emerging economy:

**Help organisations determine where human cognition adds value, where AI should assist, and what prevents the combined system from functioning effectively.**

The distinction is important.

IQ Mindware develops and evaluates individual capabilities.

SWI seeks to improve the organisational arrangements through which capabilities are deployed.

Together, they could investigate a common principle:

**Human intelligence creates strategic value when cognitive capabilities, decision policies and environmental opportunities are appropriately coupled.**

This is a Trident-G research and product-development proposition, not an empirically established combined-system advantage.

# 11. A more ambitious economic hypothesis: cognitive agency becomes a scarce strategic resource

There is a further possibility.

As analytical information becomes cheaper to produce, the relative economic importance of generating another report or forecast may decline.

What may remain difficult is deciding:

- Which interpretation of the evidence deserves confidence.
- Which strategic possibilities are genuinely feasible.
- Which competing objectives should take priority.
- Which commitments are worth making despite uncertainty.
- When to abandon an attractive but increasingly untenable opportunity.

These functions involve combinations of cognition, experience, authority and access to resources.

They cannot be reduced to raw cognitive processing capacity.

A highly intelligent employee with little decision authority may create less strategic value than their capabilities would otherwise permit.

A capable executive with poor information may make inferior decisions to a less experienced colleague with better local knowledge.

Thus, I would avoid claiming that cognitive ability alone determines economic outcomes.

A more appropriate formulation would be:

\[
\text{Realised strategic cognitive value}
=
f(\text{capability},\ \text{strategy},\ \text{knowledge},\ \text{decision rights},\ \text{niche})
\]

The critical term is **realised**.

The economy rewards cognition when it can be converted into economically consequential action.

This connects directly with your broader adaptive-agency concept.

A person or organisation may possess substantial capabilities yet have relatively few feasible alternatives.

Conversely, well-positioned actors may be able to exploit opportunities that others cannot access.

The strategic economy could intensify this asymmetry.

## 12. The MRQ–Trident-G synthesis

I would now integrate human cognition into the overall model as follows.

| Level | Strategic-economic development | Role of human cognition |
|---|---|---|
| **MRQ geopolitical system** | Competition increases pressure to secure strategic industries, technologies and resources | Strategic understanding of changing power relationships |
| **Attractor B infrastructure** | AI-enabled operating systems and industrial networks become more integrated | Operational coordination, evidence evaluation and model governance |
| **Enterprise competition** | Businesses must establish viable positions in emerging value chains | Opportunity recognition, causal reasoning and capital-allocation judgement |
| **Attractor C possibility** | People increasingly operate through—and may internalise—model-mediated decision environments | Cognitive independence, metacognition and alternative generation |
| **Trident-G developmental response** | Actors need to avoid excessive exploitation and maladaptive lock-in | Adaptive control, resilience, model reconstruction and reality-coupled learning |

The important connection is that **human cognition operates at every level, but the most strategically significant functions concern the direction and revision of action rather than the quantity of cognitive work performed**.

That suggests a potentially useful extension of the *Axes of Power* framework.

Cognitive power would not merely concern superior information processing.

It would concern the ability to construct, evaluate and act upon representations of reality while maintaining sufficient independence from those representations to recognise when they have become inadequate.

# My proposed strategic conclusion

I would express the overarching proposition this way:

> **As Western economies become increasingly organised around strategic industries, AI-enabled coordination and concentrated technological infrastructures, human cognition becomes a critical component of economic positioning and institutional governance.**
>
> Its importance lies not simply in performing the cognitive work that AI has not yet automated, but in identifying strategically consequential opportunities, evaluating competing models of reality, allocating scarce resources and determining when established commitments require revision.
>
> Organisations that develop these capabilities, and create the conditions in which they can be exercised, may be better equipped to establish durable positions within emerging economic systems.
>
> At the same time, the increasing mediation of work and decision-making through common computational models creates a potential risk of cognitive dependence and institutional lock-in.
>
> **The central strategic challenge is therefore to exploit the capabilities of AI while preserving and developing the human intelligence needed to govern objectives, evaluate reality and reconstruct strategies when circumstances change.**

### The distinction I would emphasise

**The AI economy creates a premium on better cognitive execution.**

**The strategic economy creates a need for better cognitive commitment.**

**An increasingly model-mediated economy creates a need for independent cognitive judgement.**

And the Trident-G contribution is potentially to integrate all three.

Rather than simply training people to think faster or work harder, the ambition becomes developing the capacity to **understand, commit, adapt and remain effective within changing real-world constraints**.

That is a more compelling strategic role for human cognitive development than treating it merely as a productivity enhancement.

It also suggests a distinctive positioning for IQ Mindware's broader scientific mission:

### **Develop the human intelligence required to navigate, participate in and shape an AI-powered world—rather than simply function within the models it provides.**

The research challenge is to demonstrate which component capacities and strategies can genuinely be developed, how they transfer into consequential decisions, and how much their effectiveness depends on organisational and material conditions.
